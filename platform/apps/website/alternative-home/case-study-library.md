## Case 1 — Steam: the Liverpool & Manchester Railway vs canal and coach incumbents

1. **Title:** The first inter-city railway as an organizational invention, not just a faster vehicle
2. **Organization and date/era:** Liverpool & Manchester Railway (opened 15 September 1830) vs canal operators (e.g., Erie Canal, Bridgewater Canal) and stagecoach lines; steam era, 1820s–1860s.
3. **Factual summary:** The Liverpool & Manchester Railway, opened in 1830, was the world's first inter-city railway and is described by Britannica as "the first modern railroad": the first fully timetabled service carrying both scheduled passengers and freight, relying entirely on steam locomotive traction, with all trains operated by the owner of the track — a new organizational form, not merely a steam engine bolted onto old carriage operations. It was financially successful and shaped railway development across Britain and the world. Canal incumbents responded largely within their existing model: the Erie Canal was repeatedly enlarged (1836–1862) and its tolls later reduced, but railroads offering faster shipping progressively displaced canal traffic through the late 1800s.
4. **Argument supported:** A paradigm technology rewards those who build a new operating model around it (timetables, integrated track-and-traction operation, company-run services); incumbents who improve the old model (bigger canals, lower tolls) with the old organizational logic decline anyway.
5. **Homepage section:** Section 1 (Patterns across paradigm shifts).
6. **Primary source:** "Liverpool and Manchester Railway," Encyclopaedia Britannica (continuously updated reference entry) — https://www.britannica.com/topic/Liverpool-and-Manchester-Railway ; and "Liverpool and Manchester Railway," American Society of Civil Engineers Historic Landmarks — https://www.asce.org/about-civil-engineering/history-and-heritage/historic-landmarks/liverpool-and-manchester-railway
7. **Secondary source:** "The first railways," Science and Industry Museum, Manchester — https://www.scienceandindustrymuseum.org.uk/objects-and-stories/first-railways ; "Erie Canal, Summary, Facts, Significance," American History Central (updated 2025) — https://www.americanhistorycentral.com/entries/erie-canal/
8. **Access date:** 2026-07-19.
9. **Verification note:** Directly sourced: L&MR opening date, list of organizational "firsts" (timetabling, all-steam traction, owner-operated trains), financial success (Britannica, ASCE, Science and Industry Museum). Erie Canal enlargement dates and its decline as railroads offered faster shipping are sourced to American History Central (reputable secondary). Interpretation: framing canal enlargement as "transitional adoption" and the railway's operating model as the decisive difference is Zazoo's strategic reading; the sources document the facts, not that causal frame. The oft-repeated claim that most London stagecoaches were withdrawn by the early 1840s appears in general histories but was not verified against a primary source here — do not use it as a page fact.
10. **Rights note:** No logo or media asset proposed; text only.

## Case 2 — Electricity: line-shaft "group drive" vs unit-drive factory redesign (MANDATORY)

1. **Title:** The dynamo's forty-year delay: replacing the steam engine changed little; redesigning the factory changed everything
2. **Organization and date/era:** US manufacturing at large; electrification era, ~1880–1930.
3. **Factual summary:** Warren Devine Jr.'s "From Shafts to Wires" (Journal of Economic History, vol. 43 no. 2, June 1983, pp. 347–372) recounts the shift from steam to electric power in manufacturing: between 1880 and 1930, mechanical power distribution evolved from water and steam prime movers driving shaft-and-belt systems to electric motors driving individual machines ("unit drive"). Devine's abstract states that electricity reduced the energy required to drive machinery, "but more important, enabled industry to obtain greater output per unit of capital and labor input" — i.e., the productivity gain came with the reorganization around individual-machine drive, not from swapping the prime mover under the old line-shaft layout. Paul A. David's "The Dynamo and the Computer: An Historical Perspective on the Modern Productivity Paradox" (American Economic Review, vol. 80 no. 2, 1990, pp. 355–361) uses this history to explain why a general-purpose technology's measured productivity payoff is delayed by decades until factories, layouts, and work organization are redesigned around it — his explicit analogy for the computer era's productivity paradox.
4. **Argument supported:** The core Zazoo pattern in its most-cited academic form: substituting the new technology into the old structure ("like-for-like" replacement of the central engine, keeping line shafts) yields little; redesigning the production system around the technology's native logic (unit drive, single-story layouts, workflow-driven machine placement) yields the transformation.
5. **Homepage section:** Section 1 (Patterns across paradigm shifts).
6. **Primary source:** Warren D. Devine Jr., "From Shafts to Wires: Historical Perspective on Electrification," The Journal of Economic History, Cambridge University Press, 43(2): 347–372, June 1983 — https://www.jstor.org/stable/2120827 (abstract and bibliographic record verified via https://econpapers.repec.org/article/cupjechis/v_3a43_3ay_3a1983_3ai_3a02_3ap_3a347-372_5f02.htm ); Paul A. David, "The Dynamo and the Computer: An Historical Perspective on the Modern Productivity Paradox," American Economic Review 80(2): 355–361, May 1990 — https://www.jstor.org/stable/2006600
7. **Secondary source:** "Gains from factory electrification: Evidence from North Carolina, 1905–1926," Explorations in Economic History (2025), which builds directly on Devine (1983) and David — https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/exehis/v96y2025ics0014498325000014.html
8. **Access date:** 2026-07-19.
9. **Verification note:** Both mandated sources verified to exist with the exact titles, journals, years, volumes, and page ranges claimed. Devine's published abstract directly supports the claim that the decisive gain was output per unit of capital and labor via the move to motors driving individual machines. David (1990) is verified as the canonical "productivity paradox" treatment (JSTOR 2006600; confirmed in his Wikipedia bibliography and by 1,200+ citations); its specific delayed-payoff argument is widely characterized in the citing literature as requiring "changes in factory design, operational workflows, and workforce training" before gains appeared. Full paper texts sit behind JSTOR access; the summary here relies on the published abstracts and the citing academic literature, which is sufficient for the claims made. Interpretation: the phrase "little gain from swapping engines under line shafts" is the standard reading of Devine/David in economic history — accurate in substance, but page copy should attribute it to these papers rather than state raw numbers not present in the abstracts.
10. **Rights note:** No logo or media asset proposed; text only.

## Case 3 — Electricity: Ford Highland Park (MANDATORY, companion to Case 2)

1. **Title:** Highland Park: the factory rebuilt around electric drive and flow
2. **Organization and date/era:** Ford Motor Company, Highland Park Plant, Michigan; opened 1910; moving assembly line from October 1913.
3. **Factual summary:** Ford's Highland Park plant, designed by Albert Kahn, opened in 1910 with its own on-site power plant. On 7 October 1913 it became the first factory to assemble automobiles on a moving assembly line; Model T production went "from hundreds a day to thousands a day" (Ford's corporate history), assembly time per car fell from roughly 12.5 hours to about 93 minutes, and the price of a Model T fell from $850 toward $260, with Ford's $5/day, eight-hour wage following in January 1914. Economic historians (Devine 1983; David 1990 — Case 2) treat this generation of purpose-built, electrically driven plants as the exemplar of redesigning production around electricity's native logic — machines placed in workflow order rather than under line shafts.
4. **Argument supported:** The transformational half of the electricity contrast: the famous productivity explosion happened inside a plant organized around electric unit drive and continuous flow — a new organizational design, not an old mill with new motors.
5. **Homepage section:** Section 1 (Patterns across paradigm shifts).
6. **Primary source:** "Highland Park" plant history, Ford Motor Company corporate site (published 11 March 2021) — https://corporate.ford.com/articles/history/highland-park.html
7. **Secondary source:** "Ford Highland Park Plant," Encyclopedia of Detroit, Detroit Historical Society — https://www.detroithistorical.org/learn/online-research/encyclopedia-of-detroit/ford-highland-park-plant ; "Ford Implements the Moving Assembly Line," Library of Congress Business History guide — https://guides.loc.gov/this-month-in-business-history/October/Ford
8. **Access date:** 2026-07-19.
9. **Verification note:** Directly sourced: 1910 opening, October 1913 moving assembly line, hundreds-to-thousands daily output, price decline, $5 day, on-site power plant (Ford corporate history; Detroit Historical Society; Library of Congress). The 728-to-93-minute assembly-time figure circulates via reputable secondary accounts (cited on the plant's National Historic Landmark literature) — usable with "roughly." Interpretation: identifying Highland Park specifically as the unit-drive exemplar comes from the electrification literature (Case 2 sources), not from Ford's own history page; page copy should keep the electricity linkage attributed to that literature.
10. **Rights note:** No logo or media asset proposed; text only.

## Case 4 — Computing: American Airlines SABRE vs manual reservations (MANDATORY)

1. **Title:** SABRE: redesigning the reservation business around real-time computing
2. **Organization and date/era:** American Airlines with IBM; project launched 1960, network completed December 1964; computing era.
3. **Factual summary:** Before SABRE, American's booking was almost entirely manual: teams of operators sorted rotating card files, and completing one reservation took about 90 minutes on average. American committed roughly $40 million to a joint development effort with IBM (drawing on IBM's SAGE air-defense work) to build the Semi-Automated Business Research Environment, linking reservation desks nationwide to a central processing center over 10,400+ miles of telephone lines. By the mid-1960s SABRE handled 7,500 reservations an hour and cut processing from ~90 minutes to seconds; when completed in December 1964 it was the largest civil real-time data processing system in the world. Competitors followed years later (Delta's DELTAMATIC and Pan Am's PANAMAC in 1965; United's Apollo offered to travel agents in 1976), and after 1978 deregulation the reservation system became a strategic distribution and pricing platform under CEO Robert Crandall, eventually spun out as Sabre Corporation in 2000.
4. **Argument supported:** American did not automate the card file — it rebuilt the reservation function as a real-time network and then ran new businesses (travel-agent distribution, revenue management) on top of it; computing as organizational redesign versus computing as faster clerks.
5. **Homepage section:** Section 1 (Patterns across paradigm shifts).
6. **Primary source:** "Sabre," IBM official corporate history — https://www.ibm.com/history/sabre
7. **Secondary source:** "SABRE, American Airlines' computerized reservation system" (c. 1964 artifact), Computer History Museum — https://www.computerhistory.org/revolution/artifact/176/702 ; "Computer reservation system," Wikipedia (for the industry timeline of following systems) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_reservation_system
8. **Access date:** 2026-07-19.
9. **Verification note:** Directly sourced: manual-process baseline, ~90-minute booking time, ~$40M commitment, SAGE lineage, 10,400 miles of phone lines, 7,500 reservations/hour, seconds-level processing (IBM history). "Largest civil data processing system" at 1964 completion: Computer History Museum and Wikipedia (CHM notes SABRE was influential but not the first computerized reservation system — Trans-Canada's ReserVec ran bookings by 1963; keep that nuance if the page claims "first"). Interpretation: the "transitional vs transformational" framing (later adopters automating vs American building a platform business) is a strategic reading; the sourced facts are the dates, capacities, and follow-on systems.
10. **Rights note:** No logo or media asset proposed; text only.

## Case 5 — Internet commerce: Barnes & Noble (transitional) vs Amazon (transformational) (MANDATORY)

1. **Title:** The bookstore chain that added a website vs the company built as one
2. **Organization and date/era:** Barnes & Noble, Inc. vs Amazon.com, Inc.; internet commerce era, 1994–2010s.
3. **Factual summary:** Amazon was founded in 1994 as an online-only bookseller; its March 1997 S-1 registration statement described it plainly as "the leading online retailer of books," noting average daily visits grew from ~2,200 in December 1995 to ~80,000 in March 1997. Amazon went public in May 1997 at $18 per share. Barnes & Noble was then the largest US bookstore chain — in 1998 it operated 483 superstores plus 528 mall bookstores (B. Dalton, Doubleday, Scribner's) with $2.8 billion in sales — and added its e-commerce site, BN.com, in May 1997, later run as a separate joint venture with Bertelsmann. The store chain remained Barnes & Noble's core; the online unit never overtook Amazon, which expanded from books into general retail and cloud computing. Barnes & Noble survived (unlike Borders, liquidated 2011), shrank below 600 stores by 2023, and has been rebuilding under new ownership since 2019.
4. **Argument supported:** The incumbent adopted the internet as an additional channel bolted onto a store-centric operating model; the entrant designed its entire operating model (inventory, catalog, logistics, later platform businesses) around the internet's native logic. Adoption kept Barnes & Noble alive; redesign made Amazon the defining company of the era.
5. **Homepage section:** Section 1 (Patterns across paradigm shifts).
6. **Primary source:** Amazon.com Form S-1, filed with the SEC 24 March 1997 — https://ir.aboutamazon.com/sec-filings/sec-filings-details/default.aspx?FilingId=1249014 ; "Barnes & Noble History," Barnes & Noble Inc. official corporate history (documents the May 1997 launch of BN.com) — https://www.barnesandnobleinc.com/about-bn/history/
7. **Secondary source:** "A look back in IPO: Amazon's 1997 move," TechCrunch, 28 June 2017 (quotes the S-1 directly) — https://techcrunch.com/2017/06/28/a-look-back-at-amazons-1997-ipo/ ; "Barnes & Noble, Inc.," Encyclopedia.com company profile (1998 store counts and revenue) — https://www.encyclopedia.com/economics/economics-magazines/barnes-noble-inc
8. **Access date:** 2026-07-19.
9. **Verification note:** Directly sourced: S-1 filing date and quoted language, 1997 IPO at $18, BN.com launch May 1997 (Barnes & Noble's own history page), 1998 store counts and revenue, Bertelsmann joint-venture structure, sub-600 stores by 2023 and the current 700+-store recovery (Barnes & Noble's January 2026 press release, barnesandnobleinc.com). Interpretation: attributing the divergent outcomes to operating-model design rather than channel timing is Zazoo's argument — both companies launched web bookstores within months of each other, which is precisely why the case isolates operating model rather than timing. Honest nuance: Barnes & Noble did not die; it lost the era's growth to Amazon but remains the largest US bookstore chain, and is currently expanding again.
10. **Rights note:** No logo or media asset proposed; text only.

## Case 6 — Internet entertainment: Blockbuster (transitional) vs Netflix (transformational) (MANDATORY)

1. **Title:** Nine thousand stores vs a subscription built for the network
2. **Organization and date/era:** Blockbuster Inc. vs Netflix, Inc.; internet entertainment era, 1997–2011.
3. **Factual summary:** Netflix was founded in 1997 by Reed Hastings and Marc Randolph as a DVD-by-mail service, moved to an online subscription model in 1999, and by 2000 offered flat-fee unlimited rentals with no due dates or late fees. In 2000 Blockbuster declined an offer to buy Netflix for $50 million (per Hastings' own account). Blockbuster reached its peak around 2004 with roughly 9,000 stores worldwide and about 60,000 employees, its revenue model still anchored in stores and late fees. Netflix introduced streaming in 2007 and rebuilt itself around it. Blockbuster filed for Chapter 11 on 23 September 2010, listing $1.02 billion in assets against $1.46 billion in debt; its assets were bought by Dish Network in April 2011, and the last corporate stores closed in 2014, leaving a single franchise store in Bend, Oregon. Netflix became a global streaming service in 130+ countries.
4. **Argument supported:** Blockbuster added online rental to a store-centric model whose economics (real estate, late fees) it could not abandon; Netflix designed its subscription model around distribution technology and then re-designed itself again for streaming. Same era, same industry, opposite organizational responses.
5. **Homepage section:** Section 1 (Patterns across paradigm shifts).

## Case 7 — Internet/mobile photography: Kodak (transitional) vs Instagram (transformational) (MANDATORY)

1. **Title:** The company that invented the digital camera vs thirteen people who built for the phone
2. **Organization and date/era:** Eastman Kodak Co. vs Instagram; digital/mobile photography era, 1975–2012.
3. **Factual summary:** In 1975 Kodak engineer Steven Sasson built the first self-contained portable digital camera — an 8-pound prototype recording 0.01-megapixel black-and-white images to cassette tape — and Kodak patented the electronic still camera in 1978. Sasson later recounted that executives responded that "no one would ever want to look at their pictures on a television set." Kodak did invest substantially in digital over the following decades (digital cameras, sensors, a 1,100-patent digital-imaging portfolio it tried to sell in 2011–12), but its profit engine remained film and printing. Kodak filed for Chapter 11 on 19 January 2012, listing $5.1 billion in assets and $6.75 billion in debts; at its early-1980s peak it had employed about 130,000 people worldwide. Three months later, in April 2012, Facebook agreed to acquire Instagram — a mobile-native photo-sharing company with 13 employees, founded in 2010 — for approximately $1 billion in cash and stock.
4. **Argument supported:** Owning the invention is not the same as reorganizing around it. Kodak adopted digital technology into an organization designed to sell film and prints; Instagram designed a tiny organization entirely around mobile, networked photography. The value migrated to the organization shaped like the new paradigm.
5. **Homepage section:** Section 1 (Patterns across paradigm shifts).
6. **Primary source:** "Facebook to Acquire Instagram," Facebook/Meta Newsroom, 9 April 2012 — https://about.fb.com/news/2012/04/facebook-to-acquire-instagram/ ; "Kodak files for Chapter 11 bankruptcy," ABC News / Rochester Democrat and Chronicle, 19 January 2012 — https://abcnews.com/Business/kodak-files-chapter-11-bankruptcy/story?id=15392485
7. **Secondary source:** James Estrin, "Kodak's First Digital Moment," The New York Times Lens blog, 12 August 2015 (Sasson's first-person account and the 1975 prototype details) — https://archive.nytimes.com/lens.blogs.nytimes.com/2015/08/12/kodaks-first-digital-moment/ ; "Facebook's Instagram acquisition: the billion-dollar story," The Verge, 13 April 2012 (13 employees) — https://www.theverge.com/2012/4/13/2946785/facebook-instagram-acquisition

9. **Verification note:** Directly sourced: 1975 Sasson prototype and its specifications, the 1978 patent (US 4,131,919), the executives' quoted reaction (Sasson via NYT), Chapter 11 date and asset/debt figures, the 1,100-patent sale attempt, peak employment (~130,000 worldwide, early 1980s), the $1B Instagram agreement and 13-employee headcount. **Honest nuance, required on any page use:** the popular "Kodak ignored digital" story is contested — Kodak invented the digital camera, invested billions in digital products, and was at times a leading US digital-camera seller; the failure was in profitably reorganizing its business model around digital economics, not in missing the technology. Interpretation: the Kodak-vs-Instagram pairing contrasts organizational shape, not managerial awareness; do not state that Kodak "didn't see it coming."

## Case 8 — Mobile: Nokia (transitional) vs Apple (transformational)

1. **Title:** The handset leader that iterated vs the computer company that redefined the phone
2. **Organization and date/era:** Nokia Corporation vs Apple Inc.; mobile era, 2007–2014.
3. **Factual summary:** On 9 January 2007 Apple announced the iPhone, explicitly framing it as three products in one — "a revolutionary mobile phone, a widescreen iPod... and a breakthrough Internet communications device" — with an entirely new multi-touch software interface; Apple organized the product around software, an app ecosystem (from 2008), and internet services rather than around telephony hardware. Nokia, then the world's largest handset maker, continued to iterate its Symbian-based smartphone lines, then switched to Windows Phone in 2011 under CEO Stephen Elop. On 3 September 2013 Microsoft announced it would acquire substantially all of Nokia's Devices & Services business for EUR 5.44 billion; the deal closed 25 April 2014, ending Nokia's run as an independent phone maker.
4. **Argument supported:** The mobile paradigm rewarded the organization built around software platforms and ecosystems; the incumbent that adopted smartphones as an evolution of its handset business — and even switched operating systems — exited the market within seven years of the iPhone.
5. **Homepage section:** Section 1 (Patterns across paradigm shifts).

## Case 9 — Cloud: Netflix's cloud-native rebuild on AWS

1. **Title:** Seven years to rebuild, not relocate: Netflix and the cloud
2. **Organization and date/era:** Netflix, Inc.; cloud era, August 2008 – January 2016.
3. **Factual summary:** After a major database corruption in August 2008 halted DVD shipping for three days, Netflix decided to move from vertically scaled single points of failure in its own datacenter to distributed systems in the cloud, choosing AWS. In its February 2016 announcement, Netflix stated it deliberately chose "the cloud-native approach, rebuilding virtually all of our technology and fundamentally changing the way we operate the company" — migrating from a monolithic application to hundreds of microservices and denormalized NoSQL data models — rather than lifting existing systems as-is, which would have "moved all the problems and limitations of the data center along with it." The migration completed in early January 2016 after seven years; over that period streaming membership grew 8x, viewing grew three orders of magnitude, and in January 2016 Netflix switched on service in 130+ additional countries.
4. **Argument supported:** A first-party, explicitly reasoned statement of the transitional-vs-transformational choice inside one company: "lift and shift" (adoption) was considered and rejected in favor of organizational and architectural redesign around the cloud's native model — and the redesign is what the company says enabled its global scaling.
5. **Homepage section:** Section 1 (Patterns across paradigm shifts).
6. **Primary source:** Yury Izrailevsky, Stevan Vlaovic and Ruslan Meshenberg, "Completing the Netflix Cloud Migration," Netflix company blog, 11 February 2016 — https://about.netflix.com/news/completing-the-netflix-cloud-migration
7. **Secondary source:** "Netflix Cloud Migration Complete," InfoQ, 22 March 2016 — https://www.infoq.com/news/2016/03/Netflix-Cloud-Migration
8. **Access date:** 2026-07-19.
9. **Verification note:** Directly sourced: all dates, the 2008 incident, the cloud-native-vs-lift-and-shift reasoning (Netflix's own words), 8x membership growth, 1000x viewing growth, 130-country expansion. Interpretation: none needed — this is one of the rare cases where the company itself articulates the thesis. Note Netflix still runs its own content-delivery network (Open Connect); the "everything in the cloud" claim applies to compute/data systems, not video delivery.
10. **Rights note:** No logo or media asset proposed; text only.

## Case 10 — Cloud: Microsoft's reorganization under Nadella

1. **Title:** "Mobile-first, cloud-first": reorganizing a giant around the next platform

## Case 23 — Internet reference publishing: Encyclopaedia Britannica (transitional) vs Wikipedia (transformational)

1. **Title:** 244 years of print vs an encyclopedia organized around the network
2. **Organization and date/era:** Encyclopaedia Britannica, Inc. vs Wikipedia / Wikimedia Foundation; internet era, 1994–2012.
3. **Factual summary:** Britannica, in print since 1768, digitized early — LexisNexis (1981), CD-ROM (1989), first online edition (1994) — but for years these were digital editions of the same expert-authored, periodically revised product sold under the same publishing model. 4. **Argument supported:** Britannica adopted each new medium (CD-ROM, web) while keeping the expert-publisher operating model; Wikipedia redesigned encyclopedia production itself around the internet's native logic — massively parallel volunteer authorship, instant revision, zero price. ## Case 24 — Internet classifieds: newspapers (transitional) vs Craigslist (transformational)

1. **Title:** A $19.6 billion revenue line vs a free listings site with a tiny staff
2. **Organization and date/era:** US local newspapers (classified advertising) vs Craigslist; internet era, 1995–2010.
3. **Factual summary:** Classified advertising was the profit anchor of American local newspapers: $19.6 billion in 2000, about 40% of the industry's $48.7 billion total ad revenue (Newspaper Association of America data). Craigslist, founded by Craig Newmark in 1995 as a San Francisco email list, expanded city by city offering free (or, in a few categories, cheap) classified listings with near-zero marginal cost. Newspaper classified revenue fell to $9.9 billion by 2008 and roughly $6 billion by 2009 — under a third of its 2000 level. The peer-reviewed study by Robert Seamans and Feng Zhu ("Responses to Entry in Multi-Sided Markets: The Impact of Craigslist on Local Newspapers," Management Science 60(2): 476–493, 2014) exploited the timing and geography of Craigslist's city-by-city entry to show that newspapers most reliant on classified revenue suffered larger drops in classified-ad rates after entry, with knock-on effects (higher subscription prices, lower circulation, lower display-ad rates), and estimated Craigslist's entry produced $5.0 billion (year-2000 dollars) in savings to classified-ad buyers during 2000–2007. Newspapers responded with their own online classified sites and joint ventures — digital versions of the paid-listings model — which, per the historical record, did not restore the revenue line.
4. **Argument supported:** The incumbents moved their existing paid-classifieds product online (transitional adoption); Craigslist redesigned the classified market itself around internet economics — free, centralized, network-effect listings needing almost no organization at all. A structurally different operating model, not superior execution of the old one, captured the market.
5. **Homepage section:** Section 1 (Patterns across paradigm shifts).
6. **Primary source:** Robert Seamans & Feng Zhu, "Responses to Entry in Multi-Sided Markets: The Impact of Craigslist on Local Newspapers," Management Science 60(2): 476–493, February 2014, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2013.1785 — https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2013.1785 (abstract and figures verified via https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v60y2014i2p476-493.html and the authors' working-paper PDF)
